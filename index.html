Hybrid veneer pairs a genuine wood face with a stabilised engineered backing. The result is flatter, more uniform and easier for carpenters to handle, with far less wastage. Hybrid sheets also accept polish and PU finishing just as well as natural veneer, so once the wall is complete, most guests cannot tell them apart from a distance; the difference lives in how each sheet was made. How it's made

How decorative veneer is manufactured: from log to finished sheet

From log selection and slicing to drying, grading and flitch matching â€” the process behind every good sheet. 2 min readRead

People are often surprised by how much work sits inside a one-millimetre sheet. Decorative veneer manufacturing is a patient, staged process, and the quality of each stage decides how the final panel looks on your wall.

It starts with log selection. Species, diameter, straightness and figure are assessed â€” a log with tight, even grain will produce calmer sheets, while a wild root log becomes dramatic burr veneer. This single decision sets both the look and the price band of the final sheet, because a clean, uniform log yields far more top-grade material than a knotty one. The log is then cut to length, steamed or soaked to soften the fibres, and mounted on a slicer or lathe.

The cutting method shapes the grain you see:

  • Plain slicing produces the familiar cathedral arches.
  • Quarter and rift cutting give straight, linear grain, popular for modern interiors.
  • Rotary cutting peels the log like paper and is common for economical veneers.
  • Half-round cutting is a middle path, showing broad crowns.

Next, sheets are dried to around 8 to 10 percent moisture â€” critical in a humid state like Gujarat, because damp veneer can bubble after pasting. They are clipped to size, sorted by shade and figure, and assembled into flitches: the matching sets that keep a wall's pattern continuous. The flitch is then joined, backed or pressed onto ply as needed, sanded, and inspected for knots, splits and colour. Grading is the last quality gate: clear, uniform sheets are separated from those with defects or colour streaks, and that grade is what you are ultimately paying for.

A good dealer protects all of this before you see it â€” storing bundles flat, in a dry interior, away from walls and direct sun. At Laxmi Veneer & Timber LLP, Nikol, we grade, store and match veneer with this process in mind, so sheets arrive flat, dry and true to the set you reserved.

Timber for frames and furniture: teak, sal and how to choose

Teak, sal and seasoned timber: how to choose the skeleton that keeps wardrobes and doors straight for decades. 2 min readRead

Veneer is the face; timber is the skeleton. Behind most beautiful wardrobes, doors and furniture is a frame of solid wood, and the species and seasoning of that wood decide whether the piece stays straight for decades or starts twisting within a year.

Teak is the benchmark. It is dense, naturally oily and resistant to moisture and insects, which is why it remains the first choice for main door frames, window sections and premium furniture. Sal (sali) is harder and heavier, takes load well and costs noticeably less than teak, making it a workhorse for furniture frames, planking and structural work. Beyond these, several local hardwoods are used for frames and joinery where budget matters.

Seasoning matters even more than species. Timber must be dried â€” kiln or controlled â€” to a low moisture level before use. Unseasoned wood shrinks after installation, opening joints, cracking surfaces and lifting veneer. In Gujarat's humidity swings, buying seasoned, properly stored timber from a serious supplier saves you from repairs later â€” it is the difference between frames that stay square and doors that do not drop.

  • The inside of cabinets can be laminate while the shutters are veneer â€” a very common hybrid that saves money without losing the look.
